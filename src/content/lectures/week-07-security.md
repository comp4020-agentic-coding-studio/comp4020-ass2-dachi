A deadbolt and a chain lock look like they belong to the same category of
object. One resists a shoulder; the other resists almost nothing, and mostly
exists so the person behind the door feels like they checked. Doors are full
of hardware like this — heavy-looking hinges on the unlocked side, a keypad
next to a door that's propped open all day, a push bar that announces "alarm
will sound" and hasn't been wired to anything in years. None of it is lying,
exactly. It's performing a promise the actual mechanism doesn't keep.

This matters for a redesign for the same reason accessibility does: a fix
that looks more secure without being more secure has fooled precisely the
person who trusted it most. The proposal due next week asks you to hold both
lenses on the same door — does it work for someone who isn't you, and does it
tell the truth about what it actually does.

## Outline

- security theatre: hardware that performs a promise it doesn't keep
- reading a lock or closer for what it actually resists, not what it implies
- signage that asserts a rule with nothing behind it
- holding this lens and week 5's access lens on one proposal at once
